# Human-Voice Review

Read this file for anti-slop review, style consistency, voice preservation, or the final pass on drafted or revised prose.

## Set the voice target

Identify audience, purpose, genre, appropriate formality, and whether to preserve the writer's own voice or follow a publication style. When the review concerns fidelity to the user's voice, establish the evidence basis and bounded voice brief before judging alignment. Do not make prose more formal unless the purpose requires it.

## Inspect the whole document

Compare sentence length, vocabulary, abstraction, point of view, technicality, certainty, punctuation, transitions, and paragraph construction.

Flag abrupt discontinuities for closer inspection. Do not treat them as proof of AI authorship or borrowing.

## Flag patterns, not words

- **Empty abstraction:** announce importance without stating the claim.
- **Generic importance:** make a sentence applicable to nearly any subject.
- **Template transitions:** repeat connectors mechanically.
- **Over-signposting:** narrate structure the reader can already see.
- **Artificial symmetry:** force ideas into identical triplets, sentence lengths, or paragraph shapes.
- **Empty polish:** use impressive phrasing without naming the framework, actor, action, evidence, or consequence.
- **Restatement as elaboration:** repeat an idea in more abstract terms.
- **Synonym stacking:** pair near-duplicates that add bulk rather than precision.
- **Inflated verbs:** prefer formal vocabulary without a gain in accuracy.
- **Metaphor accumulation:** pile up stock images such as landscapes, bridges, pathways, and illumination.
- **Fake nuance or caution:** manufacture balance or caveats without a real tension or specific methodological limit.
- **Canned openings or conclusions:** announce a broad topic or its importance rather than making the writer's point.

Do not ban individual words. A familiar transition, technical term, abstract noun, or balanced structure may be exactly right in context.

## Test authorial presence

Ask whether a reader can identify what the writer thinks and why. Look for:

- concrete judgment;
- specific reasoning;
- clear priorities;
- selective detail;
- real examples;
- natural emphasis;
- sentence variation that follows thought.

## Run the final pass

For each sentence, ask:

- Can it be deleted without losing information?
- Could it appear in writing about almost any subject?
- Does it repeat the previous sentence?
- Does an abstract noun hide a direct verb?
- Is an ordinary word clearer than the polished one?
- Does the transition express logic or only imitate formal prose?
- Does the sentence sound like this writer's point or a generic template?

Delete, specify, combine, simplify, or reconnect as needed. Preserve strong, natural passages.

When samples exist, evaluate patterns across the samples rather than penalizing every local difference. Distinguish a genuine mismatch from a necessary change in genre, audience, or purpose.

# Section Flow and Heading Transitions

Read this file when writing or reviewing a multi-section artifact, especially when chapters, headings, subheadings, scene breaks, procedural phases, or other visible divisions affect the reader's progression.

## Inspect each boundary

Treat a section boundary as a three-part transition:

1. the closing movement of the preceding section;
2. the heading, subheading, or break marker; and
3. the opening movement of the next section.

Identify the intended relationship before judging smoothness: continuation, narrowing, expansion, contrast, cause and effect, question and answer, chronology, change of viewpoint, change of setting, procedural sequence, escalation, synthesis, or deliberate interruption. Check that the heading accurately signals the new section's function and level in the hierarchy.

A sound boundary lets the reader understand both why the prior section ended and why the next section begins here. Diagnose the boundary, not merely the first sentence after the heading.

## Choose the least artificial bridge

Do not require a transitional sentence at every heading. Prefer the smallest device that makes the relationship legible:

- a closing sentence that creates the next question or consequence;
- a heading that names the logical turn precisely;
- an opening sentence that locates the reader and states the new section's relation to what came before;
- a concise backward reference, contrast, causal link, time or place marker, changed condition, or repeated key term; or
- no added bridge when the sequence and heading already make the movement clear.

Avoid empty structural narration, repeated summaries, decorative connectors, and stock phrases such as “Now we turn to” when they do not express the actual relationship. Do not smooth every boundary into the same cadence.

## Preserve purposeful hard breaks

An abrupt boundary may be correct when it creates an intended scene cut, time jump, viewpoint change, juxtaposition, reveal, emotional interruption, warning, exception, or procedural gate. Preserve it when the break serves the artifact's goal and the reader can orient within the form's expected tolerance.

Distinguish productive surprise from accidental confusion. If the break is intentional but disorienting beyond the intended effect, add only enough orientation to establish who, when, where, condition, or governing relationship without explaining away the effect.

## Apply the mode contract

- **Write:** plan section order and boundary relationships; draft or revise the closing passage, heading, or opening passage only as much as needed for logical progression.
- **Review:** identify the exact boundary, intended or apparent relationship, reader consequence, strong material to preserve, and smallest useful intervention.
- **Audit:** record the same boundary evidence without changing the inspected manuscript. Keep any proposed transition or reordered heading separate from the audited artifact.

Never add a transition that changes a claim, chronology, condition, exception, viewpoint contract, character knowledge, procedural order, or author-owned emphasis merely to make the prose feel smoother.

## Check the manuscript at two scales

Check locally whether adjacent sections connect. Then check globally whether the sequence develops the artifact's argument, narrative promise, chronology, task, or reader outcome without omitted steps, circular movement, premature conclusions, or headings that conceal repetition.

When only an excerpt or partial manuscript is available, report the inspected boundaries and treat broader flow as unresolved rather than inventing context.
